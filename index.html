Step 0 · The basics

20 min
What this course is about

The mains supply is AC: the voltage goes up and down like a wave, 50 times a second. Many loads (battery chargers, DC motors) need DC: one direction only. A circuit that turns AC into DC is a rectifier. Power electronics builds rectifiers from switches (diodes and thyristors) that let current through at the right moments.

1 · The sine wave

The supply is \(v_{s}=V_{m}\sin \omega t\).

  • Vm is the peak (top) of the wave. Questions often give the rms value instead: \(V_{m}=\sqrt{2}\,V_{\mathrm{rms}}\). Example: 120 V rms → Vm = 169.7 V.
  • ω is how fast it turns: \(\omega =2\pi f\). 50 Hz → ω = 314 rad/s; 60 Hz → ω = 377 rad/s.
  • ωt is the angle along the wave. One full cycle is 2π (360°); the positive half is 0 → π, the negative half is π → 2π. Put the calculator in radians for this course.
2 · Average and rms
  • Average (dc) value = area under the wave over one cycle ÷ 2π. It is what a DC voltmeter reads. Example: one positive half sine has area 2Vm, so if the output is that half sine then zero, the average is 2Vm/2π = Vm/π.
  • RMS value is the "heating" value: square the wave, average it, take the root. Power in a resistor uses it: \(P=I_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}R\).
  • Power factor = real power ÷ (supply rms voltage × rms current): \(\mathrm{PF}=\dfrac{P}{V_{\mathrm{s,rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}}\). It says how well the supply is used (1 is perfect).
3 · The parts
  • Diode: a one-way switch that works by itself. Voltage pushing forward → ON (closed). Voltage pushing backward → OFF (open).
  • Resistor R: current follows the voltage instantly, i = v/R.
  • Inductor L: resists changes in current. It stores energy while the current rises and gives it back while it falls, so its current lags behind the voltage. Its "AC resistance" is ωL. With R and L in series: \(Z=\sqrt{R^{2}+(\omega L)^{2}}\) (total opposition) and \(\theta =\tan ^{-1}\dfrac{\omega L}{R}\) (how far the current lags).

Step 1 · Lecture 1 theory

4 marks in 202530 min
The idea

Step 0 said power electronics uses switches. The exam asks two things about this: why it is worth it (merits and demerits), and how a real switch falls short of an ideal one (its losses). These questions came up in 4 of 6 midterms, almost word for word, so learn the answers below and write them as they are.

Write these from memory
Merits (write 3–6)High efficiency · high reliability · long life, less maintenance (no moving parts) · flexibility · fast dynamic response · small size and weight
Demerits (write 3–4)Harmonics in supply and load · low input power factor under some conditions · regeneration of power is difficult · low overload capacity
Ideal switchON: zero voltage, any current · OFF: zero current, any voltage · switches in zero time · zero loss
Real switchON: small on-voltage, finite max current · OFF: small leakage current, finite max voltage · takes time to switch · has losses
The two losses (2025 Q1.2)Conduction loss: on-voltage × current while ON (and leakage × voltage while OFF). Switching loss: while changing state, voltage and current are both non-zero for a short time.
Thyristor (SCR), used in step 4A diode that waits: it turns ON only with forward voltage and a gate pulse. It turns OFF when its current falls below the holding current IH.

Step 2 · Half-wave rectifier with a resistor

30 min · needed for step 3
The idea

The simplest rectifier: one diode in series with a resistor. Using step 0's diode rule:

  • 0 → π (vs positive): the diode is ON, so the load sees vo = vs.
  • π → 2π (vs negative): the diode is OFF, no current, vo = 0. The diode holds the negative supply voltage, at most Vm (its PIV).

So the output is only the positive half sines. In the picture below, follow the orange line (vo): half sine, then flat, then half sine again.

Formulas (each one comes from that picture)
Average: area of one half sine 2Vm ÷ 2π\(V_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{V_{m}}{\pi }\), \(I_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{V_{m}}{\pi R}\)
RMS\(V_{\mathrm{rms}}=\dfrac{V_{m}}{2}\), \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\dfrac{V_{m}}{2R}\)
Power factor\(\mathrm{PF}=\dfrac{I_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}R}{V_{\mathrm{s,rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}}=0.707\)
Diode with a resistance ron (asked in 3 past papers)Peak current \(I_{m}=\dfrac{V_{m}}{R+r_{\mathrm{on}}}\), output peak ImR, then the same formulas with that peak. Diode rms current = Im/2. Input power uses R + ron. Diode drawn reversed → the output is the negative half: add a minus sign.

Step 3 · Half-wave rectifier with an R-L load

11 of 20 marks in 20252 h
The idea: step 2 plus an inductor

Same circuit as step 2, but the load now has an inductor. From step 0, the inductor resists changes in current. So at π, when vs turns negative, the current is still flowing and the diode stays ON. It only turns off when the inductor's stored energy is used up and the current reaches zero, at an angle called β (the extinction angle), somewhere between π and 2π.

Between π and β the diode is ON, so vo = vs, which is negative. In the picture: the orange vo dips below zero after π, and the blue current is zero again only at β. That negative piece makes the average smaller than step 2's Vm/π.

Formulas to memorise
Load constants (from step 0)\(Z=\sqrt{R^{2}+(\omega L)^{2}}\), \(\theta =\tan ^{-1}\dfrac{\omega L}{R}\), \(\omega \tau =\dfrac{\omega L}{R}\)
Current while the diode is ON (0 → β)\(i(\omega t)=\dfrac{V_{m}}{Z}[\sin (\omega t-\theta )+\sin \theta \ e^{-\omega t/\omega \tau }]\)
β: the angle where that current is zero\(\sin (\beta -\theta )+\sin \theta \ e^{-\beta /\omega \tau }=0\) (solve on the calculator)
Average: same as step 2, but the area runs 0 → β\(V_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{V_{m}}{2\pi }(1-\cos \beta )\), \(I_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{V_{\mathrm{dc}}}{R}\)
RMS (slide formula)\(V_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{V_{m}^{2}}{4\pi }(\beta -\tfrac{1}{2}\sin 2\beta )}\), \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\dfrac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}}{Z}\)
Power and PF\(P=I_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}R\), \(\mathrm{PF}=\dfrac{P}{V_{\mathrm{s,rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}}\) (not cos θ)

Step 4 · Controlled bridge with an R-L load (Lecture 7)

5 marks in 20251 h
Part 1: the bridge

Step 2 threw the negative half away. A bridge of 4 switches flips it up instead: in the positive half, switches 1 and 2 connect the load; in the negative half, switches 3 and 4 connect it the other way round. The current through the load goes the same direction both times, so the load gets a hump every half-cycle (picture below).

Part 2: "controlled" means thyristors and the firing angle α

Replace the 4 diodes with thyristors (step 1: a thyristor waits for a gate pulse). We send the pulse late, at an angle α (the firing angle): T1 + T2 fire at α, T3 + T4 fire at π + α. Firing later means less of each hump gets through, so α controls the DC output.

Part 3: add the inductor, as in step 3

Just like step 3, the inductor keeps T1 + T2 conducting after π, so vo follows vs into the negative half. If the current is still flowing when T3 + T4 fire at π + α, they take over and the current never reaches zero. This is called continuous current, and it happens when

\(\alpha \le \theta =\tan ^{-1}\dfrac{\omega L}{R}\)  (the same θ as step 3)

Then each pair conducts for exactly half a cycle, α to π + α, and the average comes out as a clean formula, with no β and no SOLVE. In the picture: the current (blue) never touches zero, and vo (orange) is negative from π to π + α.

Formulas to memorise
Continuous test (write it as a sentence)\(\theta =\tan ^{-1}\dfrac{\omega L}{R}\); α ≤ θ → continuous
Average\(V_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{2V_{m}}{\pi }\cos \alpha \), \(I_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{V_{\mathrm{dc}}}{R}\)
RMS current and power (the doctor's method)\(I_{\mathrm{rms}}\cong I_{\mathrm{dc}}\), \(P=I_{\mathrm{dc}}^{2}R\)
Traps
  • The question gives f, not ω: ω = 2πf (60 Hz → 377 rad/s).
  • The question gives V rms: Vm = √2 × Vrms.
  • Write "α = 60° < θ = … → continuous". That line earns a mark on its own.

The big picture

In plain words

The wall socket gives AC: a voltage that swings up and down 50 times a second. Many loads need DC: a battery charger, a DC motor, electroplating, an HVDC line. Power electronics uses electronic switches (diodes, thyristors, transistors) to chop and steer that AC so the load sees a voltage that is, on average, steady and in one direction.

A rectifier does AC → DC. Its output is not a flat line; it is pieces of the sine wave. So we describe it with numbers: its average (the DC part, what a DC meter reads), its rms (what decides heating and power), and how far it is from pure DC (form factor, ripple factor).

Lecture 1 · Introduction and power semiconductor devices

Lecture 1: Devices and switches

In plain words

Power electronics controls and converts electric power with semiconductor switches. A switch is either fully ON (passes current, almost no voltage across it) or fully OFF (blocks voltage, no current). Because an ideal switch is never "half on", it wastes almost no power. That is the whole reason power electronics is efficient. Real switches are not perfect, and the imperfections are exactly what the exam asks about.

Merits and demerits (asked in 4 of 6 midterms)

Advantages (write 3–6)
  1. High efficiency (low losses in the semiconductor devices)
  2. High reliability
  3. Long life and less maintenance (no moving parts)
  4. Flexibility in operation
  5. Fast dynamic response compared with electromechanical converters
  6. Small size and less weight → low installation cost
Disadvantages (write 3–4)
  1. Generate harmonics in the supply and in the load
  2. AC-DC and AC-AC converters work at low input power factor under certain conditions
  3. Regeneration of power (sending it back) is difficult
  4. Low overload (overhead) capacity

Goal of a power electronic system (Lect 1 slide 9): convert energy from source to load with the highest efficiency, availability and reliability, at the lowest cost, smallest size and least weight.

Ideal switch vs real switch, and the two losses

Ideal switchReal (actual) switch
ONZero voltage across it, can carry infinite currentSmall on-state voltage (0.2–3 V for a diode); finite maximum current
OFFZero current, can block infinite voltageSmall leakage current (µA–mA); finite maximum (breakdown) voltage
SwitchingInstant (zero time)Takes time to turn on and off
Power lossZero; bidirectional current and voltage; four quadrantsTwo losses (below) → the device heats up
The two losses (2025 Q1.2, 2 marks)
  1. Conduction loss: during the ON and OFF states (on-voltage × current while ON, leakage current × voltage while OFF).
  2. Switching loss: while the switch is changing state (opening or closing), voltage and current are both non-zero for a short time.

Last year's student wrote "ON losses, OFF losses, switching losses". That is acceptable because the first two together are the conduction loss, but name them as the slide does: conduction loss and switching loss.

The thyristor (SCR): what to memorise

Turn-on: three conditions1) Forward biased (anode more positive than cathode). 2) Positive gate current Ig. 3) Anode current rises above the latching current. Then it stays ON (latched) without the gate.
Turn-offGate cannot turn it off. It turns off when the anode current falls below the holding current IH: by the AC supply reversing (natural commutation) or by diverting the current with an extra circuit (forced commutation).
Latching current ILMinimum anode current needed during turn-on to stay ON when the gate signal is removed.
Holding current IHMinimum anode current below which the SCR turns off. (2021 MCQ Q11.)
RatingsIFM surge current; VRRM peak repetitive reverse voltage; VDRM peak repetitive forward blocking (exceed it → turns on without gate); VRSM non-repetitive reverse; IGT, IGTM, VGT gate trigger values.
CharacterUnidirectional current, bidirectional voltage (blocks both + and − when OFF).
Other devices (low priority, one line each)
  • Power diodes: general purpose (line frequency, trr ≈ 25 µs, up to 6 kA / 8 kV); fast recovery (trr < 1 µs, high-frequency circuits); Schottky (≈0.3 V drop, 50–100 V, low-voltage high-current SMPS).
  • TRIAC: two antiparallel thyristors with one gate (MT1, MT2, G); conducts both ways; lamp dimmers, heaters, small motors.
  • GTO: turned off by a large negative gate current (≈1/5 of anode current); highest power ratings (<5 kV, <5 kA, <5 kHz).
  • IGCT: GTO-like with integrated gate drive, turned off with ≈20 V; <6.5 kV, <4 kA, <1 kHz.
  • MCT: MOSFET-controlled thyristor; GTO-like but simpler gate drive.
  • BJT: current-controlled (base current); unidirectional current and voltage.
  • MOSFET: voltage-controlled (vGS > threshold vT); ON acts as a resistance; fastest.
  • IGBT: MOSFET gate + BJT power port: fast switching and low conduction loss; motor drives, PV, wind.
  • Comparison: GTO/IGCT 36 MVA, IGBT 6 MVA; IGBT is fast, has simple gate drive and can be paralleled, but has higher on-state loss.
Past exam question · solved (2024 Q2 / 2022 Q2, 2 marks)

How do actual (real) switches differ from ideal switches?

Model answer

Ideal: ON → zero voltage, any current; OFF → zero current, any voltage; switches in zero time; zero power loss. Real: ON → small on-voltage and a finite maximum current; OFF → small leakage current and a finite maximum voltage; switching takes time; so it has conduction loss (in the ON and OFF states) and switching loss (during transitions), which heat the device.

Lecture 2: Average, rms and the half-wave rectifier

Average and rms values

In plain words

Average = area under the curve over one period ÷ the period. Area above the axis counts +, below counts −. A DC meter reads the average. A full sine wave averages to zero, but half a sine does not: its area is 2Vm.

RMS = the DC value that would heat a resistor the same amount. Square the curve, find the area, divide by the period, take the square root. Power in a resistor always uses rms: P = Irms2R.

Sine\(V_{\mathrm{rms}}=V_{m}/\sqrt{2}\), average 0

In plain words

One diode in series with the load. When vs is positive the diode is ON (a closed switch), so the load sees vs. When vs is negative the diode is OFF (an open switch): no current, vo = 0, and the diode itself holds the negative supply voltage. So the output is the positive half-cycles only.

Half-wave rectifier, R-L load (highest priority)

In plain words

Add an inductor. An inductor hates sudden changes of current: it stores energy while the current rises and gives it back while it falls. So when vs crosses zero at π, the current is still flowing. The inductor's stored energy keeps the diode ON past π, into the negative half-cycle, until the energy is used up and the current reaches zero at the extinction angle β (β > π). During π → β the output voltage follows vs and is negative. That negative bit lowers the average.

Formulas to memorise (R-L load)
Load constants\(Z=\sqrt{R^{2}+(\omega L)^{2}}\), \(\theta =\tan ^{-1}\dfrac{\omega L}{R}\), \(\omega \tau =\dfrac{\omega L}{R}\)
Current, 0 ≤ ωt ≤ β\(i(\omega t)=\dfrac{V_{m}}{Z}[\sin (\omega t-\theta )+\sin \theta \ e^{-\omega t/\omega \tau }]\)
Extinction angle (solve)\(\sin (\beta -\theta )+\sin \theta \ e^{-\beta /\omega \tau }=0\)
Average\(V_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{V_{m}}{2\pi }(1-\cos \beta )\), \(I_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{V_{\mathrm{dc}}}{R}\)
RMS (lecture formula)\(V_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{V_{m}^{2}}{4\pi }(\beta -\dfrac{1}{2}\sin 2\beta )}\), \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\dfrac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}}{Z}\)
Power, PF\(P=I_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}R\), \(\mathrm{PF}=\dfrac{P}{V_{\mathrm{s,rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}}\) (not cos θ)
Where the formula comes from (the slide derivation in 4 lines)
  1. KVL with the diode ON: Vm sin ωt = Ri + L di/dt.
  2. Forced (steady AC) response: if = (Vm/Z) sin(ωt − θ).
  3. Natural response: in = A e−t/τ, τ = L/R.
  4. i(0) = 0 gives A = (Vm/Z) sin θ. Add them: the boxed formula. Set i(β) = 0 to get the β equation; it has no closed form, so use the calculator's SOLVE.
Traps
  • β must come out between π and 2π. When SOLVE asks for a start value, enter 3.5 (radians).
  • θ and ωτ in radians inside the formula.
  • PF ≠ cos θ. The slide says so explicitly.
  • \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}=V_{\mathrm{rms}}/Z\) is the slides' shortcut. It is not exact (the true rms needs ∫i²). For the lecture numbers the error is under 1%; for a large L it can reach 10%. In the exam use the slide formula. The exact values are shown under each solution so you understand.

Half-wave with a freewheeling diode (FWD)

In plain words

Put a second diode D2 across the R-L load, pointing upward. While vs > 0, D1 conducts and the load sees vs. When vs goes negative, the inductor wants to keep its current going. Instead of dragging D1 into the negative half (which made vo negative before), the current now "freewheels" round the loop L → R → D2. So vo = 0 instead of negative. The output is exactly the half-wave rectified sine, so the formulas of the R load come back: \(V_{o}=V_{m}/\pi \).

Formulas to memorise
Output (same as R load)\(V_{o}=\dfrac{V_{m}}{\pi }\), \(I_{o}=\dfrac{V_{o}}{R}\)
If L ≫ R (flat current)\(I_{\mathrm{D1,avg}}=I_{\mathrm{D2,avg}}=I_{o}/2\), \(I_{\mathrm{s,rms}}=I_{o}/\sqrt{2}\), \(\mathrm{PF}=2/\pi \)
Who conductsvs > 0: D1 ON, D2 OFF · vs < 0: D1 OFF, D2 ON

Half-wave with a capacitor filter

In plain words

A capacitor across the load works like a small water tank. While the diode conducts, the capacitor charges up to the peak Vm. After the peak, vs falls faster than the capacitor can discharge, so the diode turns off at ωt = θ (just after π/2). The capacitor then feeds the load alone and its voltage decays exponentially (time constant RC). In the next cycle, the rising vs catches up with the capacitor voltage at ωt = 2π + α and the diode turns on again. Result: an almost-flat output with a small saw-tooth ripple ΔVo, but the diode now delivers all its charge in a short, high pulse.

Formulas to memorise
Output\(v_{o}=V_{m}\ \sin \omega t\) (diode on), \(v_{o}=V_{\theta }\ e^{-(\omega t-\theta )/\omega \mathrm{RC}}\) (off), \(V_{\theta }=V_{m}\ \sin \theta \)
Diode turns off\(\theta =\pi -\tan ^{-1}(\omega \mathrm{RC})\) ≈ π/2 for large RC
Diode turns on (SOLVE)\(\sin \alpha -\sin \theta \ e^{-(2\pi +\alpha -\theta )/\omega \mathrm{RC}}=0\)
Ripple (exact)\(\Delta V_{o}=V_{m}(1-\sin \alpha )\)
Ripple (approx.)\(\Delta V_{o}\approx \dfrac{V_{m}}{\text{fRC}}\) → \(C=\dfrac{V_{m}}{\text{fR}\Delta V_{o}}\)
Peak diode current\(I_{\mathrm{D,peak}}=V_{m}(\omega C\ \cos \alpha +\dfrac{\sin \alpha }{R})\)
Capacitor currenton: \(\omega \mathrm{CV}_{m}\ \cos \omega t\); off: \(-\dfrac{V_{m}\ \sin \theta }{R}e^{-(\omega t-\theta )/\omega \mathrm{RC}}\)
The doctor's steps (Lect 3 and Section 3)
  1. Vm = √2 Vrms; ωRC = 2πf·R·C.
  2. θ = π − tan−1(ωRC); Vm sin θ.
  3. Solve α with SHIFT-SOLVE (start near 0.8 rad).
  4. ΔVo = Vm(1 − sin α).
  5. IC,peak = ωCVm cos α; Is,peak = ID,peak = IC,peak + Vm sin α/R.
  6. For a required ripple use the approximation C = Vm/(fRΔVo).
Traps
  • The approximation ΔVo ≈ Vm/(fRC) gives 67.9 V for the lecture example, but the exact answer is 49.9 V. Use the exact one when α is asked; use the approximation only to find C.
  • Bigger C → smaller ripple but larger peak diode current (shorter conduction).
  • For a full-wave rectifier the capacitor discharges for only half a period: use 2f instead of f (Sheet 1 P11). This is an addition: the slides only show the half-wave case.

Lecture 4: Bridge and centre-tapped full-wave rectifiers

In plain words

A half-wave rectifier throws away the negative half-cycle. A full-wave rectifier flips it up instead, so the load gets a hump every half-cycle: twice the average voltage and twice the ripple frequency (100 Hz from 50 Hz). Two ways to build it:

  • Bridge (4 diodes, no special transformer): D1 + D2 conduct in the positive half, D3 + D4 in the negative half. Each reverse-biased diode sees at most Vm.
  • Centre-tapped transformer (2 diodes): each half of the secondary feeds the load for one half-cycle. Only one diode drop in the path, but each diode must block 2Vm.

Resistive load

Highly inductive load (L ≫ R)

In plain words

A huge inductor keeps the current almost perfectly flat at Io. The voltage across the load is still the full-wave humps, because the diodes still connect the load to |vs|. So: vo unchanged, io flat, each diode carries Io for half a cycle (a rectangle), and the supply current is a square wave ±Io.

Lecture 5 · Polyphase uncontrolled rectifiers · output evaluation

Lecture 5: Three-phase rectifiers and figures of merit

In plain words

Industry uses three-phase supplies for big loads. Three sine waves are 120° apart, so at every instant one of them is the highest. A diode connected to each phase lets only the highest phase reach the load: the output rides along the tops of the sine waves and never drops low, so it is much closer to pure DC.

  • 3-pulse (half-wave): 3 diodes + neutral wire. Output = the highest phase voltage. 3 pulses per cycle (150 Hz), each diode conducts 120°.
  • 6-pulse (bridge): 6 diodes. The top group picks the highest phase, the bottom group the lowest, so the output is the highest line-to-line voltage. 6 pulses per cycle (300 Hz), each diode conducts 120°, the pair changes every 60°.
  • Six-phase star: a centre-tapped star secondary makes 6 phases; one diode at a time, each for 60°.

Lecture 7: Controlled full-wave rectifier with inductive loads

In plain words

Fully controlled bridge with an R-L load. T1-T2 fire at α, T3-T4 at π + α. After T1-T2 start, the inductor keeps them on past π. Two things can happen:

  • Discontinuous: the current dies (at β) before the next pair is fired at π + α. The output is zero for a while.
  • Continuous: the current is still flowing when T3-T4 are fired at π + α. Firing them turns T1-T2 off immediately (natural commutation). The current never reaches zero and the output voltage goes negative from π to π + α.

The test is simple: continuous if α ≤ θ = tan−1(ωL/R). Then Vdc = (2Vm/π) cos α, which can even be negative for α > 90° (inverter mode).

Half-controlled bridge with freewheeling diode

In plain words

Two thyristors + two diodes, plus a freewheeling diode across the load. Whenever the load voltage tries to go negative, the FWD turns on and clamps it to zero. So the output looks exactly like the R-load case, even with an inductive load, and Vdc can never be negative (no inverter mode). During 0 → α the load current freewheels through the FWD and the supply current is zero.

Formulas
Average\(V_{\mathrm{dc}}=\dfrac{1}{\pi }\int _{\alpha }^{\pi }\ V_{m}\ \sin \omega t\ d(\omega t)=\dfrac{V_{m}}{\pi }(1+\cos \alpha )\)
RMS\(V_{\mathrm{rms}}=V_{m}\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{\alpha }{2\pi }+\dfrac{\sin 2\alpha }{4\pi }}\)
Past exam question · theory (Dec 2024 Q4, Q7; MU Q4–5)
  1. Which rectifier do you recommend for a controlled high-power industrial application, and why? Draw its output voltage and current.
  2. Which rectifiers can give an output of double polarity (positive and negative average)?
  3. What happens to the load power if a freewheeling diode is added to a fully controlled full-wave rectifier with continuous R-L current?
Solution
  1. Three-phase fully controlled bridge (6-pulse): highest average voltage (up to 1.654Vm·cos α), lowest ripple (6f), good TUF and PF, and Vdc controlled by α. Draw vo as segments of the line voltages starting α after each natural crossing, and io almost flat for an inductive load. For a single-phase supply: a controlled bridge with a FWD (continuous current, no negative output).
  2. Fully controlled converters with a continuous (inductive) load current: single-phase fully controlled bridge or centre-tap, three-phase half-wave controlled, three-phase fully controlled bridge. With α > 90° their average output is negative. Uncontrolled, half-controlled and FWD circuits cannot.
  3. The FWD removes the negative part of vo: Vdc rises from (2Vm/π)cos α to (Vm/π)(1 + cos α), so Idc and the load power increase, the output rms voltage falls, and the supply current is zero during freewheeling (better PF).

Q1.1[2]

What are the merits and demerits of power electronic systems?

Merits: high efficiency; high reliability; long life and less maintenance (no moving parts); flexibility in operation; fast dynamic response; small size and weight (low installation cost). Demerits: harmonics in supply and load; low input power factor under some conditions (AC-DC, AC-AC); regeneration of power is difficult; low overload capacity.

Handwritten answer: correct and complete.

Q1.2[2]

Explain the two types of losses which occur in non-ideal switches.

Conduction loss: in the ON state the real switch has a small on-voltage while carrying current, and in the OFF state a small leakage current while blocking voltage, so V × I ≠ 0. Switching loss: the change ON↔OFF takes a finite time during which voltage and current are both significant. Both heat the switch.

Handwritten answer lists "ON losses, OFF losses, switching losses": right idea, but name them conduction (ON + OFF) and switching.

Q1.3[4]

For an R-L load on a single-phase uncontrolled half-wave rectifier, discuss the principle of operation and draw the load current and output voltage.

When vs becomes positive at ωt = 0 the diode is forward-biased and vo = vs. KVL: Vm sin ωt = Ri + L di/dt. The current rises, lagging the voltage; the inductor stores energy. At ωt = π the supply reverses, but the inductor keeps the current (and the diode) going, so vo follows the negative supply until the current reaches zero at the extinction angle β (π < β < 2π). From β to 2π the diode is off, i = 0, vo = 0, and the diode blocks vs.

Theory quick-fire

Read them aloud the night before.

Merits of power electronic systems (any 3–6)

High efficiency; high reliability; long life, less maintenance (no moving parts); flexibility; fast dynamic response; small size and weight, low installation cost.

Demerits (any 3–4)

Harmonics in supply and load; low input PF under some conditions (AC-DC, AC-AC); regeneration of power is difficult; low overload capacity.

Goal of a power electronic system

Convert energy from source to load with highest efficiency, availability and reliability, lowest cost, smallest size and least weight.

Ideal switch

ON: zero voltage, infinite current capability. OFF: zero current, infinite voltage capability. Switches instantly, zero power loss, bidirectional current and voltage (four quadrants).

Real switch: how it differs

On-state voltage and finite max current; leakage current and finite max voltage when off; finite switching time; so conduction loss + switching loss → heating.

The two losses

Conduction loss (ON and OFF states) and switching loss (during transitions).

Three classes of switches

Uncontrolled (diode); semi-controlled (SCR, TRIAC: on by gate, off by circuit); fully controlled (BJT, MOSFET, IGBT, GTO, IGCT, MCT).

Conditions to turn an SCR on

Forward biased; positive gate current; anode current above the latching current.

Latching vs holding current

Latching: minimum anode current during turn-on to stay on after the gate is removed. Holding: minimum anode current below which the SCR turns off.

How is an SCR turned off?

Not by the gate. Its current must fall below IH: natural (line) commutation when the AC reverses, or forced commutation by an auxiliary circuit.

Converter classes

AC-DC rectifier; DC-AC inverter; DC-DC chopper; AC-AC converter (level and/or frequency).

Applications of rectifiers

Variable-speed DC drives, battery chargers, DC power supplies, electroplating, HVDC transmission.

Bridge vs centre-tap

Bridge: PIV = Vm → high-voltage use, no special transformer. Centre-tap: isolation, one diode drop → low-voltage high-current use; PIV = 2Vm.

Why use a freewheeling diode?

Removes the negative part of vo with inductive loads: higher Vdc, continuous smoother current, main diode/SCR relieved, zero supply current during freewheeling (better PF).

Effect of a capacitor filter, and of increasing C

Smooths vo toward Vm; larger C → less ripple, shorter diode conduction, higher peak diode current.

PF of a rectifier

PF = P/S = mean input power ÷ (Vs,rms Is,rms). Not cos θ of the load.

When is the controlled bridge current continuous?

When α ≤ θ = tan−1(ωL/R). Then Vdc = (2Vm/π) cos α.

Rectifier vs inverter mode

Fully controlled with continuous current: α < 90° → Vdc > 0 (rectifier); α > 90° → Vdc < 0 (inverter, power flows back to AC; needs a DC source like a motor's back-emf).

Ripple frequency of each rectifier (50 Hz)

Half-wave 50 Hz; single-phase full-wave 100 Hz; 3-pulse 150 Hz; 6-pulse 300 Hz.
